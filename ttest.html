Does Your Writing Hand Beat Your Other Hand?

What we did

For this study, each student wrote the sentence “I am amazing” for one minute with their writing hand and then for one minute with their other hand. Everyone also answered the same yes/no question about whether they write in script. I compared the class writing-hand average to Dr. Jacques’s benchmark, compared the yes and no groups on writing-hand counts, and compared each student’s writing-hand count to their other-hand count. The whole class did this in the same room on the same day.

Results

Test Mean 1 Mean 2 Difference p-value
One-sample (class writing hand vs. benchmark) 6.33 8.00 −1.67 0.0011
Independent-samples (Yes vs. No) 6.40 6.25 0.15 0.85
Paired-samples (writing hand vs. other hand) 6.33 2.56 3.78 0.000006

The class writing hand compared with the benchmark

Direction: The benchmark was higher.

Size: The class mean was 1.67 lower than Dr. Jacques’s benchmark.

Belief: The p-value was 0.0011, which is below .05.

The class wrote fewer times with their writing hand than the benchmark, and that difference was unlikely to be due to chance.

The yes group compared with the no group

Direction: The yes group was slightly higher.

Size: The yes group mean was 0.15 higher than the no group mean.

Belief: The p-value was 0.85, which is above .05.

The yes and no groups were very similar, and the difference was not strong enough to say it was unlikely to be due to chance.

Writing hand compared with other hand

Direction: The writing hand was higher.

Size: The writing-hand mean was 3.78 higher than the other-hand mean.

Belief: The p-value was 0.000006, which is below .05.

Within the same students, the writing hand produced more counts than the other hand, and the difference was unlikely to be due to chance.

In plain words

The class was below the benchmark, but students wrote more with their writing hand than with their other hand. The yes and no groups had very similar writing-hand counts, and the results do not show that the yes/no answer caused any difference.

What I would do differently

This study was useful, but it had some real limits. The class was small, so splitting it into yes and no groups left very few students in each group, which made it harder to detect a meaningful difference. Everyone wrote with their writing hand first, so practice and tiredness likely affected the second round and got mixed into the results. The data came from one room on one day, so it does not tell us much about people outside this class. Also, even when a difference appears, it does not prove that the yes/no answer caused it.

Which test would you use?

  1. Blood pressure before and after a program: Paired-samples t-test. The same patients are measured twice, before and after the program.
  2. Sleep for commuters and on-campus students: Independent-samples t-test. The question compares average sleep for two separate groups of students.
  3. County average birth weight compared with 7 pounds: One-sample t-test. One county average is being compared with a single benchmark value.
  4. Smoking and mental distress across 168 ZIP codes: Regression. The question looks at the relationship between two numeric measures across ZIP codes.
  5. Heart rate in January and June: Paired-samples t-test. The same gym members are measured at both times.
  6. Wait times at two hospitals: Independent-samples t-test. The average wait times are compared between two separate hospitals.
  7. Health insurance and smoking, both yes/no: This is the one that is different: it is not a comparison of means. Both variables are categorical, and the question asks whether they are related; that calls for a categorical association test, which we have not covered yet.
