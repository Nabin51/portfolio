What cross-sectional data can and cannot tell us

The claim
Study G shows that young adults ages 18–25 reported more frequent anxiety symptoms in 2023 (14.7%) than in 2014 (8.1%). This means that anxiety became more common in this age group over these years.

What they actually did
Every year, researchers picked a new group of about 27,000 adults to answer questions. People talked about their anxiety symptoms in the last 30 days. The researchers did not follow the same individuals from one year to the next.

Where the gap is
The study shows that the total group percentage changed, but it does not show if specific individuals became more anxious as they got older. It also cannot prove what caused the change. On top of that, the researchers changed how they asked the questions in 2019. They switched from face-to-face visits to phone calls and online forms. This change might have made people give different answers.

What cross-sectional data can tell us
Cross-sectional data is good for showing how many people have a health problem at one moment. When you repeat these surveys, you can see if the overall percentage in a group goes up or down. But it cannot show how a single person changes over time, and it cannot prove a cause.

What would have been fair
A fair conclusion is: “The percentage of adults ages 18–25 who said they had frequent anxiety went up between 2014 and 2023. However, this study cannot show if individual people changed or what caused the rise.”
